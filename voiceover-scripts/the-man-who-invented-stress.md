# The Man Who Invented Stress

**Length:** about 1,450 spoken words, roughly 10 minutes.
**Format:** a cast, not a lecture. The narrator tells the story; real people from the story step in with short lines.

Lines marked *(dramatized)* are dramatizations based on documented accounts, not verbatim quotes. A note in the video description should say so, e.g. "Some dialogue is dramatized from historical accounts."

---

## CAST

| Voice | Who | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| **NARRATOR** | The storyteller | Warm and close, like telling a friend something true at night. Not "announcer". Smiles in places. |
| **YOU** | The viewer's inner voice | Mid-20s, tired, a little breathless, honest. Quiet. Sometimes funny without meaning to be. |
| **SELYE** | Hans Selye, young scientist | Late 20s, earnest, eager, slightly clumsy energy. Light Central-European accent. Keep it subtle, no caricature. |

| **FRIEDMAN** | Meyer Friedman, cardiologist | Fast, clipped, impatient. (Friedman later called himself a classic Type A. Play it.) |
| **AD VOICE** | 1950s radio announcer | Bright, polished, too smooth. Unbranded. |

## SCRIPT

### COLD OPEN

**YOU:** [whispering, tense] It's two a.m. [pause] Why is my heart going this fast?

**YOU:** [faster] I have the meeting. And the rent. And I didn't answer her text. And—

**NARRATOR:** [softly] You know this feeling. Everyone does.

**NARRATOR:** Tight chest. Racing thoughts. That hum under everything.

**NARRATOR:** We even have one word for it.

**YOU:** [exhales] ...Stress.

**NARRATOR:** Here's the strange part. A hundred years ago, if you'd told most doctors you were "stressed"... they'd have pictured a bridge. Not a person.

**NARRATOR:** "Stress" was an engineering word. The load on a steel beam. The strain on a cable. [pause] Then one clumsy young scientist borrowed it... and gave it to all of us.

**NARRATOR:** And somebody paid for him to keep saying it.

**NARRATOR:** [beat] This is the story of the man who invented stress. And why, the way you were taught about it... might be part of the problem.

---

### ACT ONE: THE CLUMSY SCIENTIST

**NARRATOR:** Montreal. The 1930s. A young researcher at McGill University named Hans Selye is hunting for something that would make his name. A brand-new hormone.

**SELYE:** [eager, to himself] *(dramatized)* If I inject the extract... and the rats change... it's something new. It must be something new.

**NARRATOR:** Every day, he injects his rats with extract from cow ovaries. And, as the story is usually told... he's terrible at it.

**NARRATOR:** He fumbles the injections. He drops the rats. They escape. And Hans Selye ends up chasing them around the lab... with a broom.

**SELYE:** [breathless, chasing] *(dramatized)* No, no, no, come back—!

**NARRATOR:** [smiling] Months of this.

**NARRATOR:** Then he opens the rats up. And there it is. Swollen adrenal glands. A shrunken immune system. Bleeding ulcers in the stomach.

**SELYE:** [hushed, thrilled] *(dramatized)* It worked.

**NARRATOR:** He'd found his hormone. [pause] Except... he hadn't.

**NARRATOR:** Because he also had a control group. Rats he'd injected with nothing but salt water. Harmless.

**NARRATOR:** And they had the exact same damage.

**SELYE:** [quiet, deflated] *(dramatized)* ...That's not possible.

**NARRATOR:** It wasn't the extract. It wasn't anything he injected. What every rat had in common... was the experience. The needles. The dropping. The chasing. The broom.

**NARRATOR:** [slower] Their bodies weren't reacting to a chemical. They were reacting to... life going badly.

**NARRATOR:** He tested it. Cold. Exhaustion. Injury. Different ordeals. Same response, every time.

**NARRATOR:** In 1936, he published it, a short letter in the journal *Nature*. It didn't even use the word "stress" yet. That came later.

**NARRATOR:** But when it came, Selye reached for the engineering word. Pressure on a structure. Load on a frame.

**NARRATOR:** And he spent the rest of his life carrying it around the world.

---

### ACT TWO: THE WORD TAKES OVER

**NARRATOR:** It worked. Maybe too well.

**NARRATOR:** By the 1950s, "stress" was everywhere. Magazines. Medical journals. Kitchen tables.

**YOU:** [dry] So... this is his fault.

**NARRATOR:** [laughs softly] Not quite. Selye gave us a word for something real. Your body really does respond to pressure. That part was a genuine discovery.

**NARRATOR:** But a word that powerful... is useful to people. And not only to scientists.

---

### ACT THREE: THE WAITING ROOM

**NARRATOR:** San Francisco. The 1950s. Two heart doctors, Meyer Friedman and Ray Rosenman, have a waiting room problem.

**NARRATOR:** Their chairs need fixing. So they call in an upholsterer.

**UPHOLSTERER:** [looking the chairs over, puzzled] *(dramatized)* Huh. [beat] What kind of patients do you got in here, doc?

**FRIEDMAN:** [busy, clipped] *(dramatized)* Heart patients. Why?

**UPHOLSTERER:** *(dramatized)* 'Cause they only wear out the front of the seat. Nobody in here ever sits back.

**NARRATOR:** Nobody sat back. They perched. On the edge. Ready to spring up.

**NARRATOR:** As Friedman later told the story, that was the moment it clicked. Maybe the people getting heart disease... had something in common. Rushed. Driven. Impatient. Always on the edge of the seat.

**NARRATOR:** They gave it a name you've definitely heard.

**FRIEDMAN:** [crisp] *(dramatized)* Type A.

**NARRATOR:** And for a while... it looked like a breakthrough.

---

### ACT FOUR: WHO PAID FOR IT

**NARRATOR:** [darker] Now. Think about who loves the idea that heart disease comes from your personality... and your stress.

**NARRATOR:** And not from, say... smoking.

**AD VOICE:** [bright, polished] *(dramatized, unbranded)* Tense? Wound up? Long day at the office? [beat] Take a moment. Light up. Relax.

**NARRATOR:** Decades later, researchers dug through millions of pages of tobacco industry documents. And they found something.

**NARRATOR:** In 1958, Hans Selye, the father of stress, wrote to a tobacco company asking for money. He got it. A lot of it. Over the years, the industry funded his work, and it used stress research in court, to argue that something else was making people sick.

**NARRATOR:** He didn't publicly declare those ties.

**NARRATOR:** And Type A? A later study found the tobacco industry had been a major funder of that research too.

**YOU:** [quiet, stunned] ...So the cigarette companies helped sell me my stress.

**NARRATOR:** [gently] They helped sell you a story about it. That stress is a thing that happens to you. A thing that breaks you. And that you need something to take the edge off.

**NARRATOR:** [pause] And that story might be doing more harm than the stress itself.

---

### ACT FIVE: THE TWIST

**NARRATOR:** In 2012, researchers looked at data on nearly thirty thousand American adults. They'd been asked two simple questions. How much stress do you have? And do you believe stress is hurting your health?

**NARRATOR:** Then the researchers waited, and looked at who had died over the following years.

**NARRATOR:** The people with a lot of stress, who believed it was harming them... had a 43 percent higher risk of dying early.

**YOU:** [whisper] That's me.

**NARRATOR:** But here's what nobody expected. People with just as much stress... who didn't see it as harmful... didn't show that risk at all. In fact, they had some of the lowest risk in the whole study.

**NARRATOR:** [slow] Same pressure. Different story. Different outcome.

**NARRATOR:** It's one study. It can't prove the belief itself caused it. But it raises a question worth sitting with.

**NARRATOR:** What if part of what hurts us... is the way we were taught to see it?

---

### ENDING

**NARRATOR:** Hans Selye's rats got sick because their lives were being turned upside down by a clumsy man with a broom. That's real. Pressure is real.

**NARRATOR:** But think about what your body actually does. Your heart speeds up to send you more energy. Your breathing quickens to bring in more air. Your focus sharpens.

**NARRATOR:** [warmly] That's not a body breaking down.

**NARRATOR:** That's a body getting ready.

**YOU:** [quiet, slower now] It's two a.m. [breath] My heart's still going fast.

**YOU:** [a small, tired smile in the voice] ...Okay. [beat] Okay. I'm getting ready.

**NARRATOR:** [softly] The word was invented. [pause] What you do with it... is still up to you.

## SOURCES / FACT NOTES

- **Selye's 1936 letter:** "A Syndrome produced by Diverse Nocuous Agents," *Nature* 138, 32 (1936). The word "stress" does not appear in it.
  - https://www.nature.com/articles/138032a0
  - https://smpm.univ-amu.fr/sites/default/files/ressources_docs/HANS%20SELYE%20p%C3%A8re%20du%20concept%20de%20stress.pdf
- **Clumsy handling, the broom, and saline controls developing the same damage:** a widely told account, e.g. Newsweek, "Why Stress May Be Good for You." Not verified in a primary source, so the script frames it as "as the story is usually told."
  - https://www.newsweek.com/health-why-stress-may-be-good-you-82765
- **Tobacco funding of Selye** (first contact 1958; substantial funding; undeclared consultancy; stress research used in litigation): Petticrew & Lee (2011), *Am J Public Health* 101(3):411–418.
  - https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3036703
- **Type A:** Friedman & Rosenman, 1950s. The discovery was accidental and involved furniture; Friedman told the upholsterer story himself.
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meyer_Friedman
- **Tobacco industry as a major funder of Type A research:** Petticrew, Lee & McKee (2012), *Am J Public Health* 102(11):2018–2025.
  - https://researchonline.lshtm.ac.uk/id/eprint/276035
- **2012 belief study:** Keller et al. (2012), *Health Psychology* 31(5):677–684. 1998 NHIS data linked to death records. High stress plus the belief that stress affects health was associated with a 43% higher risk of premature death (HR 1.43). It's observational, which is why the script says "can't prove".
  - https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22201278/
  - https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3374921
- **"Lowest risk" line:** the script's wording that people with high stress who didn't believe it harmful had "some of the lowest risk" follows the study's reported finding. Re-check the exact wording against the full text before publishing.
