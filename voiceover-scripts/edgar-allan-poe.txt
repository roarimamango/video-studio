On October 3rd, 1849, a man was found slumped outside a tavern in Baltimore. It was election day. He was delirious, he couldn't explain where he'd been, and he was wearing someone else's clothes.

He had been missing for five days. Four days later, he was dead.

His name was Edgar Allan Poe. He invented the detective story. And he left behind the one mystery nobody has ever solved.

This is the lore behind Edgar Allan Poe.

He was born in Boston in 1809, to a family of traveling actors. His father walked out when he was a baby. When Edgar was two, his mother died of tuberculosis in a rented room in Richmond, Virginia.

A wealthy tobacco merchant named John Allan and his wife Frances took the boy in. They never formally adopted him. But they gave him a home, an education, and a new middle name. Allan. A-L-L-A-N. Not Allen. That middle name is the only thing of John Allan's that Poe would keep.

Because the two of them hated each other.

John Allan wanted a businessman. Edgar wanted to be a poet. When Poe went to the University of Virginia, Allan sent him barely enough money to survive. So Poe gambled to cover the difference, and lost. He left after one year, buried in debt, and Allan refused to pay it.

At eighteen, broke and furious, Poe did something strange. He joined the United States Army under a fake name: Edgar A. Perry. That same year, he published his first book of poems. It didn't say Edgar Allan Poe on the cover. It said, "by a Bostonian." Almost nobody bought it.

He made it to West Point. Then, when John Allan finally cut him off for good, Poe got himself thrown out on purpose. He simply stopped showing up to class, to drills, to church, until the academy had no choice but to expel him.

He was twenty-two, with no money, no family, and no name. All he had was his writing.

In Baltimore, he moved in with his aunt, Maria Clemm, and her young daughter, Virginia. In 1836, Poe married Virginia. He was twenty-seven. She was thirteen. On the marriage bond, her age was listed as twenty-one.

They were desperately poor. Poe worked as an editor, wrote reviews, and made enemies everywhere with his vicious criticism. And in between, he wrote stories nobody had ever written before.

In 1841, he published "The Murders in the Rue Morgue." A brilliant amateur named Dupin solves an impossible crime using nothing but logic. It's widely considered the first modern detective story. Decades later, Arthur Conan Doyle would admit that Sherlock Holmes owed everything to Dupin.

Poe wrote about premature burial, about a man walled up alive, about a murderer driven mad by the sound of his victim's heart, still beating beneath the floorboards. Where did all that darkness come from?

Some of it came from home.

In 1842, Virginia was singing for friends when she began to cough up blood. It was tuberculosis, the same disease that had killed Poe's mother. For five years, she got better, then worse, then better again. And Poe watched it happen.

In January 1845, he published a poem about a grieving man visited by a raven that will only say one word. "The Raven" made Edgar Allan Poe famous overnight. It was reprinted across the country. People recited it in theaters. Strangers called him "The Raven" in the street.

He was reportedly paid about nine dollars for it.

Fame didn't make him rich. And it couldn't save Virginia. In January 1847, in a freezing cottage outside New York, she died. She was twenty-four. They were so poor that, as she lay dying, her husband's old military coat served as her blanket.

After Virginia's death, Poe fell apart. He drank. He traveled. He proposed to more than one woman. And in 1849, he went back to Richmond and found his childhood sweetheart, Elmira Shelton, now a widow. They became engaged. For a moment, it looked like Edgar Allan Poe might get a happy ending.

On September 27th, 1849, he left Richmond by boat, heading north on business, planning to come back and marry her.

He never arrived.

For five days, nobody knows where he was. Then, on October 3rd, a printer named Joseph Walker found him outside a Baltimore tavern that was being used as a polling place. Walker wrote to one of Poe's acquaintances that the poet was in great distress, and needed immediate help.

Poe was taken to a hospital. For four days, he drifted in and out of delirium, hallucinating, talking to things that weren't there. According to his doctor, on his last night, he called out a name, over and over: "Reynolds." Nobody knows who Reynolds was.

On October 7th, 1849, Edgar Allan Poe died. He was forty years old. His death certificate has never been found.

So what killed him?

The most famous theory is called cooping. In nineteenth-century Baltimore, gangs would kidnap strangers on election day, get them drunk, change their clothes, and drag them from polling place to polling place to vote again and again. Poe was found at a polling place, on election day, in clothes that weren't his.

But there are other theories. Alcohol. A beating. A brain tumor. Carbon monoxide. Flu. In 1996, a doctor reviewing his symptoms argued that Poe died of rabies.

None of them can be proven.

And then the lore gets even darker. Because after Poe died, his reputation was murdered too.

A rival editor named Rufus Griswold, a man Poe had once criticized, published an obituary that began: "Edgar Allan Poe is dead. This announcement will startle many, but few will be grieved by it."

Then Griswold somehow became Poe's literary executor. He wrote a biography that painted Poe as a drunk, a madman, and a man without friends, and he forged letters to prove it.

It backfired. Griswold's lies made Poe sound exactly like one of his own characters. The tortured genius. The madman in the dark. And readers couldn't get enough.

The legend kept growing, even at his grave. For sixty years, from 1949 to 2009, every year on Poe's birthday, a mysterious figure dressed in black slipped into the cemetery in Baltimore and left three roses and a half-empty bottle of cognac. People staked out the grave for decades. Nobody ever unmasked the Poe Toaster.

So here's the lore of Edgar Allan Poe. An orphan who took his name from a man who rejected him. A poet who sold his most famous poem for almost nothing. A husband who lost everyone he loved to the same disease. A man who invented the detective story, and then disappeared into a mystery that the greatest detectives in history still can't solve.

Quoth the raven: nevermore.
