Over a hundred battles. Not a single defeat.

That's the legend of Khalid ibn al-Walid, the man they called the Sword of God. It's repeated in history books, documentaries, and a thousand comment sections: the greatest winning streak any commander has ever had.

But is it true? And how did one man go from the enemy who nearly destroyed the early Muslims, to the general who broke two empires in less than a decade?

This is the lore behind Khalid ibn al-Walid.

He was born in Mecca in the late 500s, into the Makhzum, one of the richest and most warlike clans of the Quraysh tribe. His father, al-Walid, was a powerful chief. Khalid grew up on horseback. He learned the lance, the sword, and above all, the cavalry charge.

When the Prophet Muhammad began preaching in Mecca, the Quraysh turned against him. And Khalid fought on their side.

In 625, at the Battle of Uhud, the Muslim army was winning. The Meccans were falling back. Then the Muslim archers on the hill above the battlefield left their posts to collect the spoils. Khalid saw the gap. He swung his cavalry around the hill and struck the Muslims from behind. A victory turned into a disaster.

So here's the first twist in the lore. The man famous for never losing first made his name by beating the Muslims.

A few years later, everything changed. In 629, Khalid rode to Medina and converted to Islam. His old enemies became his brothers in arms. And his first big test came almost immediately.

That same year, a Muslim army of about three thousand marched north to Mu'tah, in what is now Jordan, and ran into a far larger Byzantine force. The first commander fell. Then the second. Then the third. In the chaos, the soldiers turned to Khalid.

He couldn't win. So he did the next best thing. According to the accounts, he spent the night reshuffling his army: the front swapped with the back, the left with the right, so that at dawn the enemy saw fresh banners and fresh faces, and believed reinforcements had arrived. Then he pulled the army back across the desert, alive.

It wasn't a victory. But it saved the army. And it's said this is where he earned his title: Sayf Allah. The Sword of God.

So already, the "never defeated" claim needs an asterisk. Mu'tah was a retreat. Uhud was a win against the side he later fought for. That's why historians phrase it carefully: Khalid was undefeated in the major battles he commanded.

After the Prophet's death in 632, much of Arabia broke away. The first caliph, Abu Bakr, sent Khalid to bring the tribes back. In a series of brutal campaigns known as the Ridda wars, he won at Buzakha, and then at Yamama, one of the bloodiest battles of the era.

Then Abu Bakr pointed him at something much bigger: the Sasanian Persian Empire.

In 633, Khalid invaded Iraq. In just a few months, he fought battle after battle. At the Battle of Chains, the Persian soldiers were said to be chained together so they couldn't run. Then the Battle of the River. Then Walaja, where Khalid pulled off a double envelopment, holding the center while hidden cavalry swept around both flanks. Military historians compare it to Hannibal at Cannae. Then Ullais. Then the city of al-Hira surrendered.

Victory after victory, often against bigger armies, in a single year.

And then he got new orders. Leave Iraq. Go to Syria. Now.

The Muslim armies there were facing the Byzantine Empire, and they needed him fast. The normal route was long. So Khalid took a shortcut no army was supposed to survive: straight across the waterless Syrian desert.

The stories say his guide made the thirsty camels drink as much as they could, then tied their mouths shut so they couldn't chew the cud. Along the way, the camels were slaughtered one by one, and the water in their stomachs kept the men alive. Days later, Khalid's army appeared out of the desert, behind the Byzantine lines.

In 634, he fought at Ajnadayn and won. Then he laid siege to Damascus. And in the middle of that siege, Abu Bakr died.

The new caliph was Umar. And Umar did something shocking. He removed Khalid from overall command and put Abu Ubayda in charge.

Why? The sources disagree. Some say Umar worried that people were starting to believe the victories came from Khalid, and not from God. Others point to old tensions between the two men. Either way, Khalid stepped down and kept fighting under someone else. No rebellion. No complaint.

Which made what happened next even more remarkable.

In 636, the Byzantine emperor Heraclius gathered a huge army to take back Syria. The two sides met at the Yarmouk River. For six days, they fought in the heat and the dust. Khalid, no longer supreme commander, was given command of the field. On the final day, his horsemen drove a wedge between the Byzantine cavalry and infantry, cut off their escape, and the army collapsed.

Yarmouk was one of the most decisive battles in history. After it, the Byzantines never seriously fought for Syria again. Heraclius is said to have bid farewell to Syria forever.

Two years later, Umar dismissed Khalid for good.

And the end of the Sword of God was the one thing nobody expected. He didn't fall in battle. In 642, he died in his bed in Homs, in Syria.

According to tradition, on his deathbed he said: "I have fought in so many battles seeking martyrdom that there is no spot on my body without a scar or a wound. And yet here I am, dying in my bed like an old camel. May the eyes of cowards never sleep."

So, is the legend true?

The famous number, over a hundred battles without a loss, comes from tradition, and many of those battles were small skirmishes we know almost nothing about. Historians can't verify the count.

But here's what they can say. In the major battles Khalid commanded, against two of the greatest empires on Earth, he never lost. He won at Walaja, at Ajnadayn, at Yarmouk. He out-thought generals with bigger armies, better supplies, and centuries of military tradition behind them.

The streak might not be a hundred. But it might still be the greatest of all time.

And the man who started out as one of Islam's most dangerous enemies ended up as its most famous general. The Sword of God, who never lost, and never got the death he wanted.
