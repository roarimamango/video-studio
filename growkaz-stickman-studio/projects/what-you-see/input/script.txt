In 2006, a group of researchers at Newcastle University ran a very small experiment.

In the staff kitchen, there was an honesty box. Take a coffee, drop some money in. Nobody checks.

Every week, they taped a new picture above the price list. Some weeks, it was flowers. Other weeks, it was a pair of eyes. Just a photocopy. Just paper.

On the weeks with the eyes, people paid nearly three times as much.

Nobody was watching. Nobody knew. A picture of eyes changed what grown adults did with their own money.

So here's the question this case is really about. If a piece of paper can do that... what is your screen doing to you?

This is the lore behind what you see.

Exhibit A. The evil eye.

For thousands of years, across Mesopotamia, Greece, Rome, the Middle East and beyond, people believed a single look could hurt you. A jealous glance could sour milk, sicken a child, kill a crop.

So they fought eyes with eyes. Blue glass charms, painted on boats, hung over doors, pinned to babies. An eye to stare the evil back.

The Greeks told the darkest version. Medusa. One look at her, and you turned to stone.

It sounds like superstition. But underneath it is something every one of those cultures understood. What you look at doesn't just pass through you. It does something.

Exhibit B. Paris, 1780s.

A doctor named Franz Mesmer is the most talked-about man in the city. He claims there's an invisible force flowing through every body, and that he can control it. Patients sit in a darkened room. Mesmer stares into their eyes. He moves his hands over them. And they shake, scream, faint, and swear they're cured.

The king orders an investigation. One of the men on the commission is Benjamin Franklin.

They run a simple test. They blindfold the patients. They lie about whether Mesmer's force is being used. And the results fall apart. Patients collapse when nothing is being done to them, and feel nothing when it is.

The commission's verdict: there is no force. It's the imagination.

Mesmer was disgraced. But he'd stumbled onto something real. People didn't respond to what was happening. They responded to what they believed they were seeing.

Exhibit C. Florence, 1817.

A French writer who goes by the name Stendhal walks into the Basilica of Santa Croce. Michelangelo is buried there. Galileo. The walls are covered in frescoes.

He looks up. His heart starts pounding. He feels the life draining out of him. He has to stumble outside and sit down, afraid he's going to fall.

He wrote it all down. And over a hundred and fifty years later, a psychiatrist in Florence noticed tourists turning up at the hospital with the same symptoms. Racing hearts. Dizziness. Confusion. All after looking at art.

She gave it his name. Stendhal syndrome.

Beauty, overwhelming the body. Through the eyes alone.

So far, these are accidents. A charm, a showman, a painting. Nobody planned them.

That was about to change.

Exhibit D. New York, Easter Sunday, 1929.

In the 1920s, it's considered scandalous for a woman to smoke in public. For tobacco companies, that's half the market, lost.

So the American Tobacco Company hires a man named Edward Bernays. He's the nephew of Sigmund Freud, and he's about to invent modern public relations.

Bernays doesn't buy an ad. He stages a scene. A handful of young women march down Fifth Avenue in the Easter Parade, and on cue, they light cigarettes. Bernays has already told the press what to call them.

Torches of Freedom.

The photos run. And a taboo starts to look like liberation. Over the following decades, smoking among American women climbs.

Bernays understood something nobody had put into words before. You don't have to convince people. You just have to show them. Show it enough, and it stops looking strange. It starts looking normal.

Exhibit E. Philadelphia, 1947.

A copywriter named Frances Gerety is working late on an account for the diamond company De Beers. She's tired. She scribbles a line, almost as an afterthought.

A diamond is forever.

Over the next generation, De Beers puts diamonds in films, in magazines, in the hands of movie stars. The diamond engagement ring becomes something it had never been for most people before. Expected. A tradition.

A tradition that was, in large part, written by an ad agency.

Exhibit F. Fort Lee, New Jersey, 1957.

A market researcher named James Vicary makes an announcement that terrifies the country. He says that during a movie, he flashed two messages on the screen, too fast for anyone to consciously see.

Eat popcorn. Drink Coca-Cola.

And he says sales went up. Popcorn by more than fifty percent.

There's panic. Hidden messages. Mind control through the screen. Networks ban it.

And then, five years later, Vicary admits the study was a gimmick. The numbers didn't hold up. Some researchers doubt he ever really ran it.

The experiment was fake. But the fear was real, because people already sensed the truth. The screen wasn't just entertaining them.

It was teaching them.

Exhibit G. Stanford, 1961.

A psychologist named Albert Bandura brings children into a room, one at a time. Through a window, they watch an adult attack an inflatable clown called a Bobo doll. Punching it. Hitting it with a mallet. Yelling at it.

Then the children are left alone with the same doll.

Many of them do exactly what they saw. The same punches. The same mallet. Some of them even the same words.

Nobody told them to. Nobody rewarded them. They just watched.

Kids don't do what you say. They do what they see.

Exhibit H. The television years.

In the 1970s, a researcher named George Gerbner starts asking a strange question. Not "what's on TV," but "what does TV make people believe about the world?"

What he finds is this. The more television people watch, the more dangerous they think the world is. Heavy viewers overestimate violence. They're more afraid. More suspicious of strangers.

He called it mean world syndrome.

The screen doesn't just show you the world. It quietly replaces it.

Exhibit I. Fiji, 1995.

On the main island of Fiji, in a rural province, there is almost no television. And in traditional Fijian culture, a full, strong body is admired. Telling someone they've gained weight can be a compliment.

Then television arrives. American shows. Thin actresses. Glossy lives.

A Harvard researcher named Anne Becker is there to measure what happens next.

Three years later, she surveys teenage girls again. Before TV, none of them reported making themselves vomit to lose weight. Now, eleven percent do. The share showing signs of disordered eating has more than doubled. And the girls tell her, in their own words, that they want to look like the women on the screen.

Three years. That's how long it took for a picture on a screen to become a girl's idea of her own body.

From the screen... to the norm.

Final exhibit. Now.

Everything in this file used to need a canvas, a parade, a cinema, a living room television.

Now it lives in your pocket. And it never turns off.

In 2006, a designer named Aza Raskin invented infinite scroll. No more pages. No more bottom. No natural place to stop. Years later, he said he regretted it. His own rough guess: two hundred thousand human lifetimes, scrolled away, every single day.

In 2014, it came out that a social network had quietly run an experiment on almost seven hundred thousand of its users. For one week, it changed what they saw in their feeds. Some people saw fewer happy posts. Others saw fewer sad ones.

And people's moods followed. They started writing the way their feed felt.

They didn't know they were in an experiment. They just looked.

And now, the screen has turned the camera around. Filters smooth your skin, slim your face, brighten your eyes. Plastic surgeons started reporting something new. Patients weren't bringing in photos of celebrities anymore.

They were bringing in filtered photos of themselves.

The norm isn't a movie star now. The norm is you. Edited.

Think about the Newcastle kitchen. A photocopy of two eyes, and people changed their behaviour without even noticing.

Now think about how many hours you look at a screen every day. How many faces. How many bodies. How many opinions, outrages, products, lifestyles. All of it, quietly telling you what normal is.

There's an experiment you can do tonight. An Italian psychologist made it famous. Sit in front of a mirror in a dim room. Stare into your own eyes for ten minutes.

Most people start to see their face change. It stretches. It warps. Some see a stranger.

Because even your own reflection isn't something you just see. It's something your mind builds.

So who's building it now?

Every norm you have, you saw first.

Be wary of your eyes.
