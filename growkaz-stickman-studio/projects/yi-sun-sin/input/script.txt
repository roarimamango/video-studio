In 1597, a Korean admiral wrote to his king. The navy had just been destroyed. Almost every ship was gone. And he wrote: "I still have twelve ships."

Weeks later, with thirteen ships, he faced a Japanese fleet of more than a hundred and thirty. He didn't lose a single one.

His name was Yi Sun-sin. By the usual count, he fought around twenty-three naval battles, and never lost one. And unlike most legends, we don't have to take anyone's word for it. He wrote it down himself.

This is the lore behind Admiral Yi Sun-sin.

He was born in 1545, in Seoul, into a noble family that had fallen on hard times. He didn't start out as a sailor. He started out as a soldier.

At twenty-seven, he took Korea's military service exam. Halfway through the horseback test, his horse threw him, and he broke his leg. The story goes that he got up, stripped bark from a willow tree, splinted his own leg, and finished the test. He still failed. Four years later, he tried again, and passed.

His early career was anything but glorious. He was posted to remote frontiers. He made enemies by refusing to do favors for powerful officials. In 1587, after a raid on the northern border, he was blamed for the losses, stripped of his rank, and made to serve as a common soldier.

He worked his way back. And in 1591, a friend in the government got him an unlikely job: commander of the navy in Jeolla, Korea's southwest province.

The timing was perfect. Because across the sea, Japan had just been unified after a century of civil war, under a warlord named Toyotomi Hideyoshi. And Hideyoshi wanted more. He planned to march through Korea and conquer China.

Yi seemed to sense what was coming. He trained his sailors, stockpiled supplies, and rebuilt an old Korean design: the turtle ship. A warship covered with a roof, studded with sharp spikes so no one could board it, with cannons on every side and a dragon's head at the bow that could pour out smoke. According to his diary, he was test-firing its cannons just days before the invasion began.

In the spring of 1592, Japan invaded with an army of more than one hundred and fifty thousand men, landing at Busan. They were veterans of a hundred years of war, armed with muskets. Korea's army collapsed. Seoul fell in less than three weeks. The king fled north.

On land, Korea was losing everything. At sea, it was a different story.

Yi's fleet sailed out and caught Japanese ships at Okpo. He won. Then at Sacheon, he sent the turtle ship into battle for the first time. It smashed straight into the Japanese line, and musket fire couldn't stop it. Yi himself was shot in the shoulder. He kept fighting, and won again. Then again. And again.

Then came Hansan Island. Yi sent a few ships to provoke a large Japanese fleet, then turned and fled into open water. The Japanese chased them. And that's when Yi's whole fleet swung around into a giant half-moon, the crane wing formation, and closed around them. Most of a Japanese fleet of more than seventy ships was destroyed or captured.

It was a turning point of the war. Japan's army depended on supplies by sea, and Yi had cut the road. Their advance in the north stalled.

So you'd think Korea treated him as a hero. Instead, it nearly killed him.

Yi's rivals at court were jealous. And the Japanese fed false intelligence to the Korean government through a double agent: a Japanese fleet would be crossing at a certain spot, and Yi should attack it there. Yi suspected a trap and refused to sail. The king saw it as disobedience.

In 1597, Yi Sun-sin was arrested, dragged to the capital in chains, and tortured. He came close to being executed. Instead, for the second time in his life, he was stripped of his rank and sent to serve as a common soldier.

His replacement was his rival, an admiral named Won Gyun. Within months, Won Gyun led the fleet straight into a Japanese ambush at Chilcheollyang. The Korean navy was wiped out. Won Gyun was killed. Only about a dozen ships escaped.

The king had no choice. He brought Yi back. And he suggested that maybe Yi should give up on the navy, and fight on land instead.

Yi's reply became one of the most famous lines in Korean history: "I still have twelve ships." As long as I am alive, he wrote, the enemy will not dare to look down on us.

One more ship was found. That made thirteen.

In October 1597, a Japanese fleet of more than a hundred and thirty warships came through the Myeongnyang Strait, a narrow channel with one of the strongest tidal currents in Korea. Yi was waiting. The night before, he told his officers: "Those who seek death shall live. Those who seek life shall die."

When the battle began, the other Korean captains hesitated and hung back. For a while, Yi's flagship fought almost alone, against wave after wave of enemy ships. Then the others rejoined. And then the current turned. The Japanese ships, packed into the narrow strait, were pushed back into each other.

By the end of the day, around thirty Japanese warships had been destroyed. Yi did not lose a single ship.

It was one of the most lopsided naval victories ever recorded. And it came at a terrible price. Weeks later, in revenge, Japanese soldiers killed Yi's youngest son. In his diary, Yi wrote that he wanted to die, but had to go on living for his men.

In 1598, Hideyoshi died, and the Japanese began pulling out of Korea. Yi wasn't going to let them leave easily. In December, alongside a Chinese fleet, he attacked the retreating Japanese at Noryang.

The battle raged through the night. As dawn came, Yi was on the deck of his flagship, urging his men on with the war drum, when a bullet struck him.

His last words, according to the records, were: "The battle is at its height. Do not announce my death." His nephew hid his body, took up the drum, and kept beating it. The fleet fought on, never knowing their admiral was gone. Noryang was a victory. His last one.

So, is the streak real?

The number twenty-three depends on how you count. Some of those were small clashes, and historians argue about where one battle ends and the next begins. But here's what makes Yi different from almost every other undefeated legend: the evidence. He kept a war diary for most of the seven-year war. Today it's recognized by UNESCO as part of the Memory of the World. Together with the Korean court records, it gives us his battles, his doubts, his illnesses, and his grief, in his own hand.

By every count, he never lost a battle he commanded. He was jailed by his own king, tortured, demoted twice, lost his son, and kept winning anyway.

Thirteen ships against more than a hundred and thirty. Twenty-three battles. Zero defeats. And a man who, even in death, refused to let his side stop fighting.
