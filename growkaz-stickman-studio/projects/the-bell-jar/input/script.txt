"It was a queer, sultry summer, the summer they electrocuted the Rosenbergs, and I didn't know what I was doing in New York."

That's the first line of The Bell Jar, one of the most famous novels ever written about a young woman falling apart. It reads like fiction. But almost everything in it really happened. The hotel was real. The hospital was real. The doctors, the boyfriend, the girl herself. All real. And the woman who wrote it published it under a fake name, and never lived to see what it became.

This is the lore behind The Bell Jar.

Sylvia Plath was born in Boston in 1932. Her father, Otto, was a college professor and an expert on bees. When Sylvia was eight years old, he died. Her mother, Aurelia, was left to raise two children alone, working long hours so they could have the education she believed would save them. Sylvia took that seriously. She published her first poem at eight. By her teens, she was sending stories to magazines, collecting rejection slips, and trying again.

So in June 1953, at twenty years old, on paper, she was winning. A scholarship student at Smith College. Straight A's. Prize-winning poems. And now, one of twenty young women chosen as guest editors at Mademoiselle, one of the most glamorous magazines in America.

The magazine put them up at the Barbizon, a hotel for women in Manhattan, where men weren't allowed past the lobby. In the novel, it becomes "the Amazon." Plath's alter ego, Esther Greenwood, is supposed to be having the time of her life. Instead, she feels nothing.

Even that first line is a clue. The Rosenbergs, a couple convicted of spying for the Soviet Union, were executed in the electric chair that June. Esther can't stop thinking about it, about what it must feel like to be burned alive along your nerves. It sounds like a strange detail to open with. It isn't. Electricity is coming back in this story.

Plath's real summer looked just like Esther's. Her boss, the editor Cyrilly Abels, became the tough, brilliant Jay Cee. A luncheon where the guest editors came down with food poisoning ended up in the book. And on her last night in New York, Plath climbed to the roof of the Barbizon and threw her clothes, piece by piece, into the night air.

She came home to the suburbs of Wellesley, Massachusetts, and to bad news. She'd been rejected from a summer writing course at Harvard. With nothing to do, she couldn't write. She couldn't read. She couldn't sleep.

Her doctors' answer was electroshock therapy, given badly, and it terrified her. There's the electricity. In August 1953, Sylvia Plath disappeared. Newspapers ran her photo. Hundreds of people searched. Three days later, she was found, barely alive, beneath her family's house. She had tried to end her life.

What came next was a stroke of luck. Olive Higgins Prouty, a best-selling novelist who had funded Plath's scholarship, paid for her to go to McLean Hospital, one of the best psychiatric hospitals in the country. There, she met Doctor Ruth Beuscher, a young psychiatrist who actually listened. In the novel, Prouty becomes the wealthy Philomena Guinea, and Beuscher becomes the kind Doctor Nolan. Plath would keep writing to her real-life doctor for the rest of her life.

Other people weren't so lucky in how they appeared. Buddy Willard, the smug medical student Esther can't stand, was based on Plath's real boyfriend, Dick Norton. And Joan Gilling, Esther's unsettling double in the hospital, was based on a real woman named Jane Anderson, who would one day take it to court.

Plath recovered. She went back to Smith and graduated with highest honors. She won a Fulbright scholarship to Cambridge, in England. And at a party there in 1956, she met a young poet named Ted Hughes. Their first meeting was so intense that she bit him on the cheek. Four months later, they were married. Two ambitious poets, sharing one tiny desk. The breakdown, it seemed, was behind her.

In 1961, living in England with a baby daughter, she finally wrote it all down, in just a few months. Privately, she called it a potboiler. She reportedly said it was a book she had to write to free herself from the past.

But she also knew it would hurt people. Almost every character was a real person, thinly disguised. Her mother would recognize herself. So would her old boyfriend, her benefactor, her doctors. So when the book came out in Britain in January 1963, the name on the cover wasn't Sylvia Plath. It was Victoria Lucas.

Reviews were polite. Sales were modest. And by then, Plath's life had come apart again. Ted Hughes had left her for another woman. She was alone in London, in one of the coldest winters on record, with two small children, getting up before dawn to write the poems that would make her famous. On February 11th, 1963, less than a month after The Bell Jar was published, Sylvia Plath died by suicide. She was thirty years old.

So what is the bell jar? It's a glass dome, the kind scientists use to trap something inside, cut off from the air. Esther says that wherever she went, on the deck of a ship or at a café in Paris or Bangkok, she would be sitting under the same glass bell jar, "stewing in my own sour air."

It's depression, described from the inside. You can be anywhere, surrounded by anyone, and still be sealed off. But it's also something bigger. Esther is smart and ambitious in 1950s America, where a woman was expected to choose: career or marriage, poet or mother. In the book's most famous image, she sees her life as a fig tree. Every fig is a different future. A famous poet. A professor. An editor. A wife and mother. A life traveling the world. She wants all of them. But choosing one means losing the rest. And as she sits there, unable to decide, the figs begin to wrinkle and go black, and drop, one by one, to the ground at her feet.

Plath felt this herself. Years earlier, she had written in her journal: "I can never read all the books I want; I can never be all the people I want and live all the lives I want."

And yet, the novel doesn't end in the dark. On the last page, Esther is about to walk into a room full of doctors who will decide whether she can leave the hospital. We never find out what they say. The book stops at the door, on a note of hope.

After Plath's death, her final poems were published as a collection called Ariel, and they made her a legend. The mysterious Victoria Lucas was quickly unmasked. In 1966, the novel was republished in Britain under her real name. But in America, her mother, Aurelia, fought its release for years, arguing that it was cruel to people who had loved and helped her daughter.

She lost. Ted Hughes, who still controlled Plath's estate, approved it. In 1971, The Bell Jar was finally published in the United States, and it became a bestseller. Aurelia answered with a book of her own: hundreds of Sylvia's letters home, to show the world a warmer daughter than the one in the novel.

The book kept growing. Schools tried to ban it. A film came out in 1979, and Jane Anderson, the real woman behind Joan, sued, saying it showed her doing things she had never done. The case was settled in 1987. In 1982, Plath's Collected Poems won the Pulitzer Prize, the first time it had ever been given to a poet after their death. And The Bell Jar has never gone out of print.

Today, Esther's fig tree is all over the internet, shared by people born half a century after Plath died, who recognize themselves in a girl from 1953 who felt her life slipping past her.

Plath wrote this book to get free of the past. In the end, it couldn't save her. But it has helped millions of readers feel a little less alone under their own bell jars.
